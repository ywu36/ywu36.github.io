*The hidden pattern behind 2025's under-30 billionaire class reveals an uncomfortable truth about how trust is earned in tech.*

<!--more-->

There’s a pattern hiding in plain sight among this year’s youngest self-made billionaires.

Forbes just released their [2025 list of self-made billionaires under 30](https://fortune.com/2025/12/23/more-self-made-billionaires-under-30-than-ever-before-11-made-ultra-wealthy-club-last-3-months-ai-unemployment-entry-level-jobs-also-creating-wealth-tech-startups/)—a record-breaking 13 people, up from just 7 previously. Nine came from AI. Four from prediction markets. Look at what they did *before* they built their billion-dollar companies:

- **Tarek Mansour** (Kalshi, $1.3B) — interned at **Goldman Sachs** (equity derivatives) and **Citadel** (global macro). Once interviewed at **Jane Street**—by Sam Bankman-Fried. Co-founded the first federally regulated prediction market.
- **Luana Lopes Lara** (Kalshi, $1.3B) — spent college summers at **Bridgewater Associates** and **Citadel Securities**. Met Mansour during an internship at **Five Rings Capital**. World’s youngest self-made female billionaire.
- **Michael Truell** (Cursor, ~$1.3B) — interned at **Two Sigma** before co-founding the AI code editor Jensen Huang calls his “favorite enterprise AI service.”
- **Aman Sanger** (Cursor, ~$1.3B) — interned at **Bridgewater Associates** before co-founding Cursor.
- **Alexandr Wang** (Scale AI, $3.2B) — worked at **Hudson River Trading** before dropping out of MIT at 19 to start Scale AI. Now Meta’s Chief AI Officer.
- **Shayne Coplan** (Polymarket, $1B) — bought Ethereum at **$0.30** when he was 16. Been trading crypto for five years before founding Polymarket at 21.
- **Ed Craven** (Stake.com, $2.8B) — built virtual casinos in **RuneScape** as a teenager. Launched **Primedice** (Bitcoin gambling) in 2013—took over $1 million in bets on day one. Created the world’s largest crypto casino.
- **Anton Osika** (Lovable, $1.6B) — first technical hire at **Ampfield Aktiebolag**, a Stockholm proprietary trading firm.

What do they all have in common? **Quant trading internships at Citadel, Bridgewater, Two Sigma, Five Rings, Goldman, Hudson River Trading, Ampfield. Crypto trading. Gambling markets.**

They didn’t start with grand visions of changing the world. They started by winning zero-sum games against other smart people—and proving they could do it before anyone gave them a dollar to build something bigger.

The pattern isn’t that they all worked in finance. It’s that they all won zero-sum games before building positive-sum companies.

## The PvP Proving Ground

In gaming terms, these founders all mastered PvP—player versus player—before graduating to PvE—player versus environment.

Trading is the purest form of PvP. Every dollar you make is a dollar someone else loses. There’s no narrative, no mission statement, no founder mythology to hide behind. The scoreboard updates in real-time. You either extract alpha from the market, or the market extracts it from you.

Consider Jeff Yan’s path. At Hudson River Trading, he built systems that executed thousands of trades per second in US equities. His job was to find inefficiencies faster than anyone else and exploit them before they disappeared. When he moved to crypto and founded Chameleon Trading, his team became one of the largest market makers on centralized exchanges.

Only then did he build Hyperliquid—a decentralized exchange designed with the performance characteristics that serious traders demand. He knew what those characteristics were because he’d spent years on the other side of the trade.

This isn’t coincidence. It’s causation.

## The Trust Problem in Tech

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: tech has a trust problem.

Anyone can write a pitch deck claiming they’ll change the world. Anyone can raise venture capital on a vision. The startup ecosystem is designed to reward storytelling as much as execution. This creates an adverse selection problem—the founders who are best at narrative aren’t necessarily best at building.

Trading provides a different filter. Markets don’t care about your Stanford pedigree or your YC batch. They don’t care about your Twitter following or your thought leadership. They care about one thing: can you predict price movements better than the collective intelligence of everyone else trying to do the same thing?

When Liang Wenfeng started High-Flyer in 2015, he and his Zhejiang University classmates had been developing algorithmic trading ideas since the 2008 financial crisis. By 2021, they managed $11 billion. When Liang pivoted to AI and founded DeepSeek, he wasn’t asking anyone to take a leap of faith. He’d already proven he could build systems that outcompeted other brilliant people in the most adversarial environment imaginable.

The markets had already vetted him.

## From Solo Queue to World Boss

The Kalshi story illustrates this perfectly. Tarek Mansour interned at Goldman Sachs in equity derivatives and at Citadel in global macro trading. His co-founder Luana Lopes Lara interned at Bridgewater and Citadel Securities. They met during an internship at Five Rings Capital in 2018.

At these firms, they observed something curious: sophisticated traders were constantly betting on future events, but indirectly. They’d trade Treasury futures based on Fed rate predictions, or currency pairs based on election outcomes. But there was no clean way to simply bet on the event itself.

Their insight came from watching markets—from being inside the machine. And crucially, they’d already proven they could operate inside that machine successfully. When they pitched Y Combinator and later navigated the CFTC regulatory process, they weren’t just two kids with an idea. They were two proven traders who understood market structure from the inside.

Shayne Coplan took a different route to the same destination. At 16, he made one of the best trades of the decade—buying into Ethereum’s 2014 crowdsale at $0.30. By the time he started Polymarket at 21, he’d been operating in crypto markets for five years.

He wasn’t theorizing about prediction markets from an academic perspective. He’d read Robin Hanson and Friedrich Hayek, sure. But more importantly, he’d been a participant in the most volatile, adversarial markets in existence. When FTX collapsed, he understood exactly what decentralization needed to look like to earn trust.

## The Single-Player Prerequisite

Ed Craven’s path might be the most explicit version of this pattern. He won $6,000 playing bingo on a family cruise at age 12, became obsessed with gambling statistics, and built his empire from virtual casinos in RuneScape. By the time he launched Stake.com in 2017, he’d been studying edge and expected value for over a decade.

The Mercor founders break this pattern slightly—Brendan Foody, Adarsh Hiremath, and Surya Midha were high school debate champions rather than traders. But debate is itself a PvP game. You win by being better than your opponent in real-time, with judges scoring immediately. The Thiel Fellowship that funded them exists precisely because Peter Thiel believes in this same principle—prove yourself in competition before trying to change the world.

The exception that proves the rule might be Anton Osika at Lovable. His background is physics (CERN), engineering (KTH), and AI startups. But look closer: his previous company Depict.ai was backed by Y Combinator and Tiger Global. He’d already survived the competitive crucible of startup building and proven he could win.

## Why This Matters

The PvP-to-PvE pattern suggests something important about how trust is actually earned in tech.

We tell ourselves that the startup ecosystem is a meritocracy of ideas. The best vision wins. The most innovative technology prevails. But this narrative obscures a more fundamental truth: trust is earned through demonstrated competence in adversarial conditions.

CZ didn’t build Binance into the world’s largest crypto exchange because he had the best vision for cryptocurrency’s future. He built it because he’d spent years constructing high-frequency trading systems and understood market infrastructure at a molecular level. When he left OKCoin to start Binance in 2017, raising $15 million in an ICO, investors weren’t betting on his pitch deck. They were betting on his track record.

The same logic applies to Liang Wenfeng and DeepSeek. When he announced High-Flyer would pursue artificial general intelligence in 2023, his partners thought he was crazy—that only giants like ByteDance and Alibaba could compete. But he’d already accumulated 10,000 Nvidia A100 GPUs. More importantly, he’d proven he could build AI systems that worked in the most unforgiving testing environment possible: live markets where wrong predictions cost real money.

## The Uncomfortable Implication

There’s a reason this pattern makes people uncomfortable. It suggests that the path to building something that helps everyone starts with winning a game that helps only yourself.

Trading doesn’t create value. At best, it improves price discovery and market efficiency. At worst, it’s sophisticated extraction. The young billionaires on this list didn’t start by feeding the hungry or curing diseases. They started by taking money from other traders.

But maybe that’s precisely the point. The skills that let you win in PvP—pattern recognition, risk management, systems thinking, emotional discipline, operating under uncertainty—are exactly the skills that let you build companies capable of tackling PvE challenges at scale.

Liang Wenfeng didn’t just build a profitable hedge fund. He built a hedge fund that used AI to predict market movements, which taught him how to build AI systems that could operate in adversarial conditions, which is why DeepSeek works.

Jeff Yan didn’t just make money trading. He learned what traders actually need from exchanges, which is why Hyperliquid’s 11-person team generates more annual income than Nasdaq’s 9,000 employees.

## The Path Forward

If you’re building something ambitious, this pattern suggests a counter-intuitive strategy: find a zero-sum game you can win, and win it first.

This doesn’t mean becoming a quant trader. The game can be competitive programming, or chess, or debate, or any arena where your performance is measured against other smart people trying to beat you. The point isn’t the specific domain. The point is proving—to yourself and to others—that you can win when the stakes are real and the competition is fierce.

The world is full of people who want to skip PvP and go straight to world-saving PvE. They want to build climate tech, or cure aging, or solve alignment. Noble goals. But the market increasingly rewards founders who’ve already proven they can win adversarial games.

Because in the end, trust isn’t given. It’s earned. And the fastest way to earn it is to win a game everyone agrees is hard.
