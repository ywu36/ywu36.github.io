Abstract. Computers can now see and hear, but they still struggle to smell: smells are costly to label, and we have no shared language to describe them. Most things we smell are mixtures, and in the DREAM olfactory mixtures challenge, an open international competition, no method reliably beat a simple recipe: describe each molecule in odour words with the Principal Odor Map (open-POM), then average. Our insight is that mixing should follow how the nose combines odours: components add in a receptor-like code, and a few dominant ones outweigh the rest. We present Pony, a graph neural network trained on single molecules, with only its mixture layer then fine-tuned on DREAM; it is 18 times smaller than open-POM yet outperforms it by 37% and 13% on the DREAM challenge's T46 and V50 test sets and by 18% in cross-validation. Pony sums unit molecule directions in its hidden layer, weights each molecule by a strength read from its atom attention, and gives each mixture a learned length, so similarity depends on both direction and length. It also beats all prior methods we evaluated.

As large models for text, images, audio, and video keep improving, recognizing and classifying content in those modalities is becoming a commodity. Smell lags far behind: it is the major human sense that AI has made the least progress on. As Osmo CEO Alex Wiltschko put it, "Computers have been able to digitize vision and hearing, but not smell – our deepest and oldest sense." (Monell Chemical Senses Center, 2023)

Sight and hearing have had shared maps for centuries. Smell has not.

  • Music: In the Pythagorean tradition, going back to the 6th century BCE, the most pleasing intervals match simple ratios (2:1 for the octave, 3:2 for the fifth), as recorded by Boethius (early 6th century CE), and this number-based theory guided Western music for two thousand years. In the 19th century, physics and physiology explained why: consonance comes from how overtones line up and how the ear processes them (Helmholtz, 1863).
  • Color: In 1672, prism experiments showed that white light is a mixture of colours, and by 1704 Newton had bent the spectrum into a colour circle. In 1905, color got a full coordinate system (hue, value, and chroma) that is still used today (Newton, 1672, 1704; Munsell, 1905).

Smell only began to get a shared language in the past few decades, one industry at a time:

  • Perfume, the traditional odor map: Perfumers have long grouped scents into families such as floral, woody, and amber. The Fragrance Wheel, which places related families next to each other, first appeared in Michael Edwards' Fragrance Manual (Edwards, 1992).
  • Wine: In the 1980s, researchers built the Wine Aroma Wheel, a standard vocabulary arranged from broad categories at the center to specific terms at the rim; the 1987 revision suggests reference standards so tasters can train on the terms (Noble et al., 1984, 1987).
  • Coffee: The coffee industry published its first flavor wheel in 1995 and rebuilt it in 2016 on a scientific sensory lexicon (Spencer et al., 2016). That lexicon defines about 110 attributes for flavour (taste and aroma), texture and overall impression, with definitions and references (Specialty Coffee Association of America and World Coffee Research, 2016; World Coffee Research, 2016).

These maps are vocabularies arranged by expert panels. They help trained people name what they smell inside one domain, but they cannot predict how a new molecule will smell, and they do not carry over from one domain to another. A large-scale learned map that generalizes to new molecules came in 2023, when Lee et al. published the Principal Odor Map, a graph neural network model that maps molecular structure to perceived odour and describes new molecules at a level comparable to trained human panelists (Lee et al., 2023).

From single molecules to mixtures: the DREAM mixture challenge

Single molecules are largely mapped; mixtures are the next frontier. The Principal Odor Map predicts how a single molecule smells about as well as a trained human panelist (Lee et al., 2023). But almost nothing we smell is a single molecule: coffee, wine, and perfume are mixtures of tens to hundreds of compounds. Predicting how a mixture smells, and how two mixtures compare, is the open problem.

Early work showed mixtures can be treated as one percept. Snitz et al. (2013) found that summing a mixture's molecule vectors into a single vector, scaled to unit length, and measuring the angle between two such vectors predicts how similar people find two mixtures, while comparing components one by one does not.

The second DREAM olfaction challenge turned this into a benchmark. The organizers merged six published datasets, from similarity ratings and triangle tests, into one training set on a common 0 (indistinguishable) to 1 (maximally different) scale: 507 mixture-pair measurements in the paper's count, 730 rated rows in the released file. Over about three months, 26 teams competed, and 19 submitted final predictions for a hidden test set of 46 pairs (T46). Recently, a new validation set of 50 pairs (V50), each mixture with 9 to 11 molecules and no molecule shared within a pair, was rated to test the models prospectively (Satarifard et al., 2026).

In the published results (Satarifard et al., 2026), four teams tied at the top: D2Smell, UMich CASI, ChemSenSim Lab, and belfaction. The surprise was that mixture distances were well captured by a compact semantic space, the predicted odour words ("fruity", "sweet") of the single molecules: an ensemble that kept only such semantic features improved on the full ensemble. Predictions were pulled toward intermediate values, and errors were lowest for mixtures rated in the mid range (about 0.5 to 0.8), where training data are most abundant (Satarifard et al., 2026).

We also tested a foundation model for single molecules on this dataset: MIST. MIST (Molecular Insight SMILES Transformers) is a family of general molecular foundation models with up to an order of magnitude more parameters and data than earlier work. It is pre-trained on SMILES strings from the Enamine REAL Space with masked language modelling, then fine-tuned on more than 400 property tasks, from physiology and electrochemistry to quantum chemistry. For smell, the authors fine-tuned it on 4,983 labelled molecules from the open-POM curation of Lee et al.'s data, the same set we split into 3,975 training and 1,008 held-out molecules (AUROC 0.915 vs 0.894 reported for Google's original POM), and extended it to mixture distances, but that mixture model was not publicly released as of November 2026. We used the released fine-tuned odour model (MIST-28M encoder, 135 odour words) in place of open-POM: predict each molecule's odour words, average them over the mixture, and compare the two mixtures (Wadell et al., 2025).

But no model consistently beats open-POM. We reran each model's released code where possible and scored it against the public labels (Table 1). D2Smell, one of the top four teams, beats open-POM on T46 (r 0.469 vs 0.432) but drops to r 0.165 on V50, where open-POM reaches 0.552. MIST avg is on par with open-POM, with slightly lower r on both sets (0.407 vs 0.432 on T46, 0.547 vs 0.552 on V50). POMMix (Tom et al., 2025), a mixture model trained on 865 pairs compiled from published datasets, and PL21, a DREAM team that fitted a Lasso on mixture pairs from Bushdid et al. (2014), stay below open-POM on both sets. Both sets are small (46 and 50 pairs), and rating noise limits every model: two rating sessions of the same V50 pairs agree at only r 0.74 (Satarifard et al., 2026).

Session 1. Exploring open-POM with added factors

The open-POM baseline already captures much of what people perceive in a mixture, so before building anything new we ask what it misses. We keep open-POM frozen and change one thing at a time in how the ratings are read from it. Each change adds only a few fitted numbers, so its effect can be read off directly, and whatever helps tells us something the baseline leaves out.

1.1 What does averaging open-POM miss?

Our baseline is the open-POM (Barsainyan et al., 2023) model of the DREAM paper (Satarifard et al., 2026): simple, and hard to beat. It describes each molecule by open-POM's predicted odour-word profile, averages the profiles within each mixture, and compares the two averages by direction:

$$p(A)=\frac{1}{n_A}\sum_{m\in A} q(m),\qquad d(A,B)=\left\|\frac{p(A)}{\|p(A)\|}-\frac{p(B)}{\|p(B)\|}\right\|,\qquad \hat y(A,B) = a\,d(A,B)+c$$
  • $A$ and $B$ are the two mixtures in a pair, each a set of molecules; $m\in A$ runs over the molecules in $A$.
  • $n_A$ and $n_B$ are the numbers of molecules in each mixture.
  • $q(m)\in\mathbb{R}^{138}$ is open-POM's predicted odour-word profile of molecule $m$, with each word rank-scaled over the 235 DREAM molecules.
  • $y(A,B)$ is the rating people gave the pair, from 0 (indistinguishable) to 1 (maximally different), and $\hat y(A,B)$ is the model's prediction.

The slope $a$ and intercept $c$ are fitted by least squares on the 820 training pairs. They change RMSE but not Pearson r. It reaches r 0.392 in cross-validation, 0.432 on T46 and 0.552 on V50. It misses two things: which molecules dominate a mixture, and how the size of a mixture changes what people perceive.

1.2 Factor: chemical groups decide which molecules dominate

A plain average lets every molecule count equally. But some molecules dominate a mixture's smell, and which ones is written in their chemistry. Each molecule $m$ carries indicators $x_g(m)\in\{0,1\}$, equal to 1 if it contains chemical group $g$; we turn them into a weight $W(m)$ and replace the plain average with a weighted one. The groups are not chosen by hand. We start from RDKit's standard list of 85 functional groups (aldehyde, ester, benzene ring and so on) and count them on the molecules of the training pairs, using structure only, never ratings. 42 of the groups occur, groups carried by exactly the same molecules are merged into one, and we keep the 24 carried by at least 5 molecules. Table A2 in the appendix lists them.

$$W(m)=\exp\Big(\sum_{g=1}^{24}\theta_g\,x_g(m)\Big),\qquad p_W(A)=\frac{\sum_{m\in A}W(m)\,q(m)}{\sum_{m\in A}W(m)}$$
$$d_W(A,B)=\left\|\frac{p_W(A)}{\|p_W(A)\|}-\frac{p_W(B)}{\|p_W(B)\|}\right\|$$

$\theta_g$ is the weight of group $g$, learned from the ratings. A positive weight means molecules carrying that group pull the mixture toward their own smell; a negative weight means they recede. With every $\theta_g=0$ the model is the baseline again. The fitted weights favour benzene rings, methyls on a ring, aldehydes and aromatic nitrogen; straight carbon chains and ketones recede. The group weights raise cross-validated r from 0.392 to 0.400 and T46 from 0.432 to 0.439.

Nine broad chemical classes were too coarse, and weighting by open-POM's 138 odour words overfitted. The 24 RDKit groups strike the balance. Adding interactions between groups did not improve the fit.

1.3 Factor: mixture size, by counting molecules

The baseline's errors grow with the size mismatch between the two mixtures (r 0.180). The direct way to capture this is to count molecules and add the size mismatch to the readout:

$$\hat y(A,B) = a\,d_W(A,B) + b\,|\log n_A-\log n_B| + c$$

We also tested total size, $\log n_A+\log n_B$; it did not improve the fit and is left out. The size count alone raises cross-validated r from 0.392 to 0.418 but leaves T46 unchanged (0.432) and lowers V50 slightly (0.549); together with the group weights it reaches 0.426 in cross-validation. The 24 group weights are fitted together with $a$, $b$ and $c$, 27 numbers in all, by minimizing

$$\sum_{(A,B)\in\text{train}}\big(\hat y(A,B)-y(A,B)\big)^2+\lambda\sum_{g=1}^{24}\theta_g^2,\qquad \lambda=10^{-3}$$

1.4 Directly encoding the vector's magnitude in the distance

Counting molecules helps, but the count is an input we add by hand, and it treats every mixture of the same size alike. The averaged profile already holds this information. It is a vector with a direction and a magnitude, and the baseline divides the magnitude away and compares directions only. Yet the magnitude says how many molecules the mixture holds and how much they agree. For a mixture $A$ with weighted profile $p_W(A)$,

$$\|p_W(A)\|=\sqrt{\sum_{w=1}^{138} p_{W,w}(A)^2},\qquad h(A)=\log\|p_W(A)\|$$

Averaging unlike molecules shortens the vector toward the mean profile; molecules that agree keep it long. On real data $h(A)$ falls with the number of molecules at r −0.70 across the 800 training mixtures. We keep both the direction $\hat p_W(A)=p_W(A)/\|p_W(A)\|$ and the log-magnitude $h(A)$, and replace the molecule count with one distance:

$$d(A,B)=\sqrt{\|\hat p_W(A)-\hat p_W(B)\|^2+\eta\,\big(h(A)-h(B)\big)^2},\qquad \hat y(A,B) = a\,d(A,B)+c$$

The model has 27 numbers (24 group weights, $\eta$, $a$, $c$), all fitted on DREAM with the same loss. Size now comes out of the averaging itself, still varies between mixtures of the same size, and is combined with the direction difference in one distance. The link between prediction errors and size mismatch falls from r 0.180 with the baseline to 0.027.

1.5 Summary: what Session 1 tells us

Session 1 was a probe. Its factors are hand-crafted and ad hoc: 24 chemical groups picked from a fixed list, and a magnitude inherited from averaging word profiles, all added on top of a frozen network built for single molecules. Their value is in what they reveal: by keeping open-POM frozen and adding a few numbers at a time, each gain points to something specific. With only 27 fitted numbers they improve on the baseline in every column (Table 2), and they show what the averaging baseline misses: some molecules dominate a mixture, and which ones is written in their structure; and the size and make-up of a mixture belong in the distance, carried by the length of the mixture vector rather than by a molecule count.

These two findings led us to design Pony (Session 2). Dominance becomes a strength that the network learns from atoms, in place of hand-picked groups. The magnitude becomes a learned mixture length, in place of an inherited one. Both act in a small network shaped for combining molecules, with few numbers fitted on mixtures.

Session 2. A small network shaped for mixtures

Session 1 kept open-POM frozen and added terms around it. The terms helped, but they sit on top of a representation learned to predict the odour words of single molecules, not to combine molecules into mixtures. We first look at this limitation and where it comes from, and then replace open-POM with a network sized to the data and shaped for mixtures.

2.1 The memorization problem of the open-POM network

Open-POM was built to predict the odour words of single molecules, with 2.09 million parameters trained on 3,975 labelled molecules. A network of that size can fit its training molecules without learning rules that carry to new ones: with 1,008 molecules held out, open-POM reaches a held-out AUROC of 0.890 but scores 0.069 higher on the molecules it was trained on (Table A1). We call this train-minus-held-out difference the memorization gap. For mixtures, what matters is less single-molecule accuracy than what the molecule embedding distinguishes, since a mixture model can only combine that. In open-POM's embedding many DREAM molecules point in similar directions, so two mixtures that share no molecule can look alike because their molecules do.

The mixture data do not make up for this, and can make it worse. DREAM's 800 mixtures are built from only 234 molecules, each appearing in a median of 137 training pairs, so a flexible model can score well by recognizing molecules instead of learning how they combine. We saw this whenever more was fitted on the pairs: the more of MIST's parameters were fine-tuned on mixtures, the less the gain carried over to held-out mixtures, and fine-tuning a network on DREAM improved about as much when the ratings were randomly reassigned to pairs. Published methods show the same fragility across test sets: D2Smell falls from r 0.469 on T46 to 0.165 on V50 (Table 1).

To see which parts of open-POM's design matter, we retrained it with one component changed at a time (Table A3). No single component explains the memorization gap: from 0 to 8 message-passing rounds and from width 50 to 200 it stays between 0.064 and 0.073, and only a linear read-out lowers it, to 0.058. The clearest effect on mixtures is in pooling, the step that merges a molecule's atom vectors into one vector. Open-POM pools with set2set, in which an LSTM builds a new query over the atoms of each molecule. Replacing it with one learned query shared by all molecules, as in Pony, raised mixture r on every set (T46 0.404 → 0.457, V50 0.554 → 0.570, CV 0.397 → 0.404) with 0.88 M fewer parameters, while single-molecule accuracy stayed level (0.888 against 0.890). We suspect a shared query helps because it weights the same kinds of atoms in every molecule, so the molecule vectors stay comparable when they are combined.

2.2 How far we are from the data a deep network normally has

Deep networks need data on a scale that odour research does not have. Language models are the clearest case: every next word in a text is a free label, so they train on billions of them. Vision and protein models rely on hundreds of thousands to millions of human-labelled examples. Odour has thousands of labelled molecules and hundreds of rated mixtures.

2.3 Architecture Pony: As simple as possible, but not simpler

Our network is Pony: a Parsimonious Odour Network with hidden-laYer mixing. It is trained on single molecules only, and its layout borrows the structure of the olfactory pathway rather than its measured values.

Single molecules: a receptor-like code, translated into words. In the nose, an odorant activates a combination of odour receptors, each receptor responds to many odorants, and the identity of a smell is carried by the pattern across receptors (Buck and Axel, 1991; Malnic et al., 1999). Pony's 64-unit hidden layer plays this role: each molecule is a pattern across a small set of shared channels. A single linear layer then translates this pattern into the 138 odour words, much as a person translates a smell into language.

Mixtures: combined where the nose combines them. The molecules of a mixture reach the receptors together, and their responses do not simply add: odorants compete at shared receptors (Rospars et al., 2008; Reddy et al., 2018), and responses are normalized downstream of the receptors, as shown in the fruit fly (Olsen et al., 2010). Pony therefore combines molecules in the hidden layer, as a weighted sum of unit directions, where open-POM averages the final word profiles; the weights carry the competition and are normalized within each mixture. Finally, Pony is parsimonious: 18 times smaller than open-POM, sized to the few thousand labelled molecules it learns from (Section 2.2).

Together these choices make the additive rule plausible. A sum is only as good as the space it is taken in, and in a receptor-like code it stays close to how a mixture is first encoded. Because the read-out is linear, a weighted sum of hidden patterns is read out as the same weighted combination of the molecules' word scores, so the mixture remains interpretable in odour language. The few numbers fitted on mixtures then only decide how much each molecule counts and how mixtures are compared.

Why the simpler network helps

The same lesson has been drawn in combination prediction in biology. Ahlmann-Eltze et al. (2025) tested deep models that predict the effect of combining two genetic perturbations, including GEARS from Leskovec's group (Roohani et al., 2024) and large pretrained foundation models. None of them beat a baseline that simply adds the two single effects, and none found genetic interactions better than a baseline that predicts no change: the models mostly predicted buffering interactions and rarely synergistic ones. With few measured combinations, the combination-specific capacity of a deep model has little reliable signal to learn from.

Odour mixtures are in the same regime: thousands of labelled single molecules, 820 rated mixture pairs, and an averaging baseline that is hard to beat (Session 1). We take three consequences from that work.

  1. Keep the additive rule as the backbone of mixing. Our mixture vector is a weighted average of molecule directions. The network is not asked to learn interactions from DREAM.
  2. Put the learning where the data are. Everything the model knows about a molecule, including how much it should count, is learned from single molecules.
  3. Remove capacity that single-molecule data cannot justify. The whole open-POM network, with more message-passing rounds, wider layers, LSTM pooling and a deep dense read-out, reaches a higher held-out AUROC on single molecules than Pony (w/o attention) (0.890 against 0.860) but also a larger memorization gap (0.069 against 0.025; Table A1), without better mixture prediction (Table 6).

Where we differ from the additive baseline is the weights: which molecule counts more is a property of each molecule alone, so it can be learned from single molecules and still change the mixture (attention, below).

Following these lessons, from the memorization problem (Section 2.1), the data gap (Section 2.2) and the additive rule above, we design Pony as shown in Figure 1: open-POM on top, Pony below.

Table A1 in the appendix compares the two stage by stage, and Figure 2 (Section 2.4) shows how Pony combines molecules into a mixture. Pony keeps open-POM's input and message passing, and removes what is not needed for mixtures: two rounds of message passing, the LSTM pooling, and the deep dense read-out. Taken out of open-POM one at a time, none of these changes its mixture prediction (Table A3). Pony (w/o attention) addresses memorization: its memorization gap is 0.025, against 0.069 for open-POM (Table A1). It is small, learns from single molecules only, and is then frozen. Everything fitted on DREAM, 133 numbers per copy (Section 2.5), acts on molecular structure, never on a molecule's identity. Its middle layer is shaped for mixing:

  • Mix in the hidden layer, not in word space. A mixture is a combined pattern across the 64 hidden units.
  • A simple path to the words. The hidden layer is centred and followed by one linear layer.
  • Similarity is measured by both a learned direction and a learned length. A molecule vector's raw length was never trained to mean anything for mixtures, and keeping it when mixing made results worse (T46 0.454 against 0.467, V50 0.566 against 0.593, with attention-based molecule weights and only the slope and intercept fitted). Each molecule therefore enters a mixture as a unit direction (Section 2.4). Length is not dropped from the odour distance: each mixture gets a learned length instead, so the distance uses both direction and length.

Attention: chemical groups, encoded inside the network

In Section 1.2, dominance came from 24 predefined chemical groups. A chemical group is a small set of atoms in a particular arrangement. After message passing, each atom vector $H_j\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$ already describes its atom together with its neighbourhood. So instead of handing groups to the model, we add one small part to Pony (w/o attention), a scorer over atoms:

$$s^0_j = H_j\cdot u_0,\qquad \alpha_j=\frac{\exp(s^0_j)}{\sum_{j'\in m}\exp(s^0_{j'})}$$

Pony gives one vector of 64 numbers for each molecule. It is the representation of a single molecule: Pony computes it from that molecule alone and knows nothing about the mixture the molecule is in.

$$v(m)=\sum_{j\in m}\alpha_j H_j$$

The scorer $u_0\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$ is a learnable weight vector, shared by all atoms and trained with the rest of Pony by the same word loss, so it learns which atoms decide a molecule's odour words. Section 1.2 handed the model 24 chemical groups; here the network can find the important atom neighbourhoods itself.

In Figure 1(b), attention is the red part. Each atom vector (a blue bar) gets one score (a red circle; darker means a higher score), and the scores are the weights of the sum $\Sigma$ that turns the atom vectors into the molecule vector. Pony (w/o attention) has the same drawing with all circles equal.

The scorer is trained with the network on single molecules, so it never sees a mixture rating. Inside the network, attention only pools the atoms of each molecule; the ablation in Section 2.7 (Table 6) compares Pony without and with it.

In mixtures: rebalancing the atom scores with the mixture ratings

The scorer $u_0$ learned which atoms decide a molecule's own odour words. In a mixture the question is different: which atoms decide how strongly a molecule shows through the others. The two need not coincide. Some atoms may matter more in a blend than in a single molecule's words, and the scale of the scores, which only had to rank atoms within one molecule, need not suit a comparison between molecules. When Pony is used for mixtures, we therefore add one term that rebalances the atom scores with the distance ratings of the DREAM mixtures: a scale $\beta$ and a small correction $\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$,

$$s_j=H_j\cdot u,\qquad u=\beta\,u_0+\delta$$

$\delta$ is penalized toward zero in the loss, so at $\delta=0$ the scorer keeps the direction of the single-molecule scorer $u_0$ and $\beta$ only sets its scale; $u_0$ and the rest of Pony stay frozen. Every number here is shared by all atoms of all molecules, so the strength is computed from structure and is hard to memorize per molecule from 820 rated pairs (Section 2.1). The model can learn "atoms that look like this are strong" rather than "this molecule is strong", and a molecule it has never seen gets its score the same way.

Why no self-attention between atoms

We leave self-attention between atoms out because it would add capacity that the single-molecule data cannot justify, for little that message passing does not already provide. Each atom vector $H_j$ already takes in every atom within three bonds, which covers 71% of the atom pairs in a DREAM molecule (median 10 heavy atoms). Self-attention would add only the remaining long-range pairs, and it would be trained, like the rest of Pony, on 3,975 single molecules. Open-POM already has one step in which all atoms of a molecule interact by content, its LSTM pooling. Replacing it with a plain average over atoms leaves held-out accuracy (0.890), the memorization gap (0.069) and mixture prediction unchanged (Table A3). We therefore keep one shared scorer over atoms and leave long-range interactions between atoms for when more data exist.

2.4 An additive mixture representation: learned shares and a learned length

Pony represents a single molecule, but every DREAM example is a pair of mixtures, so the model needs a rule that turns molecular embeddings into a mixture representation. The Principal Odor Map does not supply one: its authors note that the map "does not provide clear guidance about how stimuli can be mixed to produce new percepts" (Lee et al., 2023). The open-POM model of the DREAM paper therefore combines molecules at the output, averaging their predicted word probabilities (Satarifard et al., 2026); this is our Open-POM avg. Pooling at the output has two drawbacks. Each word score is a probability, so averaging a molecule that is surely "fruity" with one that surely is not gives a middle value, the same as averaging two uncertain molecules. And the 138 words keep only what the vocabulary names.

Step 1: pool unit directions in the embedding, by addition

We pool in the embedding, the receptor-like layer of Section 2.3, so that molecules are combined where the nose combines them rather than after each has been translated into words. Because Pony's read-out is linear, a sum of embeddings is read as a sum of word scores. The same additive form, compared by angle, is what Snitz et al. (2013) and Ravia et al. (2020) found to work with hand-made molecular descriptors.

Before adding, we scale each embedding to unit length. In a sum, a molecule's pull is the norm of its embedding, and training on odour words never tied that norm to strength, so unnormalized pooling would give each molecule an accidental strength. Hand-made descriptors avoid this by sharing a common scale. Unlike the mixture magnitude of Section 1.4, which measures how much the molecules agree, this per-molecule norm carries no trained meaning.

Each molecule thus enters as its direction $\hat v(m)$, the mixture vector $z(A)$ averages the directions of the $n_A$ molecules in $A$, and two mixtures $A$ and $B$ are compared by the distance between their normalized mixture vectors. The distance is a number with no units, while ratings run from 0 (indistinguishable) to 1 (maximally different), so a linear map with slope $a$ and intercept $c$, fitted on the ratings, converts one into the other:

$$\hat v(m)=\frac{v(m)}{\|v(m)\|},\qquad z(A)=\frac{1}{n_A}\sum_{m\in A}\hat v(m)$$
$$d(A,B)=\left\|\frac{z(A)}{\|z(A)\|}-\frac{z(B)}{\|z(B)\|}\right\|,\qquad \hat y(A,B)=a\,d(A,B)+c$$

In this form every molecule of a mixture counts the same: each direction $\hat v(m)$ enters with the same weight $1/n_A$. Step 2 replaces this equal weight with a learned share $\pi_A(m)$ for each molecule.

Step 2: a learned distance between mixtures

In Step 1 the distance $d(A,B)$ is fixed: nothing in it is learned from the mixture ratings. The only two numbers fitted on the ratings are the slope $a$ and intercept $c$ that convert the distance into the rating scale, $\hat y(A,B)=a\,d(A,B)+c$. Two observations about the ratings show where this fixed distance is wrong.

Observation 1: some molecules dominate a mixture through a few key chemical groups. In Step 1 every molecule counts the same, but in a mixture odorants do not take equal turns. Odorants in a mixture partly mask one another (Cain, 1975), and people are hardly able to identify more than three odorants in a mixture of up to eight (Laing and Francis, 1989; reviewed by Thomas-Danguin et al., 2014). Part of this happens already at the nose, where one odorant can suppress the receptor response to another (Reddy et al., 2018). Which odorant wins may also depend on its company: it has been proposed that mixtures of similar odours are more likely to be perceived as one configural whole (reviewed by Thomas-Danguin et al., 2014). At the receptors, odorants that bind the same receptors compete for them (Rospars et al., 2008). Session 1 found dominance in the DREAM ratings too: the fitted group weights favour benzene rings, aldehydes and aromatic nitrogen (Section 1.2), and on their own they raise cross-validated r from 0.392 to 0.400. Which molecule dominates is written in its structure, and in how it differs from the rest of its mixture.

Observation 2: averaging exaggerates olfactory white. Large mixtures do tend to smell alike: with about 30 components of equal intensity that span odour space, most mixtures that share no molecule smell alike, a percept called olfactory white (Weiss et al., 2012). However, in the DREAM ratings this effect is mild: the rated difference falls with mixture size at r −0.233. Averaging unit directions makes it more than twice as strong, at r −0.585. The more directions are averaged, the closer the result moves to the same middle direction, so large mixtures end up close largely regardless of what they contain. The averaged model therefore calls large mixtures more alike than people do, and discards the differences between them that people still perceive. A better model should let mixture size pull mixtures together only as much as the ratings show, and keep what tells one large mixture from another.

Design objective 1: a relative weight for each molecule (from Observation 1). Dominance is about the importance of a molecule relative to the others in the same mixture. The model needs a weight per molecule, normalized within the mixture. Because the weights are shares that sum to one and the mixture vector is normalized before comparison, they decide where the mixture points; its length is set separately (Design objective 2).

Score the strength of a molecule. We have no measured strength for the molecules, and we do not want to list by hand which ones dominate, so the model learns strength from structure alone. A molecule's smell is often set by a few decisive atoms rather than by its size, such as an aldehyde or an aromatic nitrogen, groups that the weights of Section 1.2 favour. Pony already scores every atom $j$ of a molecule: $s_j=H_j\cdot u$, where $H_j$ is the atom's vector and $u=\beta u_0+\delta$ the scorer rebalanced on the mixture ratings (Section 2.3). We turn the scores of a molecule's $T_m$ atoms into one number, the molecule's strength score $\ell(m)$, by a soft maximum with temperature $\tau$:

$$\ell(m)=\tau\,\log\Big(\frac{1}{T_m}\sum_{j\in m}e^{\,s_j/\tau}\Big)$$

So $\ell(m)$ is one number per molecule, not per atom: for small $\tau$ it approaches the score of the molecule's strongest atom, $\max_{j\in m}s_j$, and larger $\tau$ lets the other atoms count more. The strength score is not a measured quantity: only its differences within a mixture matter, and they are fitted to the mixture ratings through the shared scorer $u$ and the temperature $\tau$.

This is log-sum-exp pooling, as used by Pinheiro and Collobert (2015) for weakly supervised image segmentation. There, only image-level labels are known, and the image score must come from a few decisive pixels: a hard maximum trains poorly, and an average dilutes the decisive pixels among the rest. The soft maximum lies in between, moving from the average ($\tau\to\infty$) to the maximum ($\tau\to 0$). Our setting has the same structure. Only a molecule-level effect is observed in the mixture ratings, and it comes from a few decisive atoms. The soft maximum lets those atoms set the strength, and unlike a sum it does not make a large molecule strong just because it has many atoms. The temperature $\tau$ is fitted on the ratings.

Model the relative strength of a molecule in its mixture. The strength score is computed from the molecule alone, so it cannot know the company the molecule keeps, which Observation 1 says also matters. We measure how a molecule differs from its company by leaving it out: we add the cosine between the molecule's embedding $v(m)$ and the mean embedding $\bar v_{A\setminus m}$ of the other molecules in its mixture $A$, with one fitted weight $\kappa$. Its sign is learned: $\kappa<0$ lets a molecule unlike its company stand out, $\kappa>0$ favours one that blends in.

$$\omega_A(m)=\exp\Big(\ell(m)+\kappa\,\cos\big(v(m),\,\bar v_{A\setminus m}\big)\Big)$$

Only the share of each molecule within its mixture should matter, so we normalize these weights within the mixture. The shares replace the equal weights of Step 1 and pool the unit directions $\hat v(m)=v(m)/\|v(m)\|$:

$$\pi_A(m)=\frac{\omega_A(m)}{\sum_{m'\in A}\omega_A(m')},\qquad z(A)=\sum_{m\in A}\pi_A(m)\,\hat v(m)$$

This is the same operation Pony uses inside a molecule, one level up. Inside a molecule, atom vectors $H_j$ are weighted by atom shares $\alpha_j$ into the molecule embedding $v(m)$. Inside a mixture, molecule directions $\hat v(m)$ are weighted by molecule shares $\pi_A(m)$ into the mixture vector $z(A)$.

Multiplying a unit direction by a weight is the same as giving the molecule a vector of that length. So $\pi_A(m)$ is a learned, relative length for each molecule, in place of the accidental one removed in Step 1. Figure 2 shows how a molecule enters its mixture, including the mixture length of Design objective 2.

Design objective 2: an absolute length for each mixture (from Observation 2). How close a mixture sits to olfactory white is a property of the mixture as a whole. The shares of Objective 1 cannot express it: they are normalized within the mixture, so they only decide where the mixture points. Averaging hides it as well, because every large mixture is pulled toward the same middle direction and normalizing to unit length then discards how far it was pulled. The model needs a length for each mixture, read from the direction that Objective 1 produces.

Model the mixture length. Session 1 already found that a mixture's magnitude belongs in the distance (Section 1.4). The simplest candidate, Pony's own vector length, does not help: used as the length, it moves cross-validated r only from 0.445 to 0.450, because it was never trained to carry information about mixtures. The length has to be learned.

To see what a learned length changes, picture each mixture as a point in the embedding. In Step 1 every point lies on the unit sphere (Figure 3a), and the straight-line distance between two points on the sphere depends only on the angle between them, $\|\hat z(A)-\hat z(B)\|^2=2-2\cos\big(\hat z(A),\hat z(B)\big)$. We now let each point slide outward or inward along its own direction, to a length $g(A)$ (Figure 3b). Since the length should follow from what the mixture is, we compute it from the mixture's direction, through one learned vector $\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$ shared by all mixtures:

$$\hat z(A)=\frac{z(A)}{\|z(A)\|},\qquad g(A)=\exp\big(\gamma\cdot\hat z(A)\big)$$

A mixture that points along $\gamma$ gets a length above one and moves outward; one that points away from $\gamma$ gets a length below one and moves inward; the exponential keeps every length positive. Two mixtures are then compared by the ordinary Euclidean distance between the moved points:

$$d(A,B)=\big\|\,g(A)\,\hat z(A)-g(B)\,\hat z(B)\,\big\|$$

Why should the same angle count differently in different places? Human odour space is not uniform. The main axis of perceived differences between odours is pleasantness (Khan et al., 2007), and perceptual descriptions of odours are better fitted by a three-dimensional hyperbolic geometry than by a flat Euclidean one (Zhou et al., 2018). Mixing adds its own pattern: a strong component can mask the quality of the others (Cain and Drexler, 1974; reviewed by Thomas-Danguin et al., 2014), and large blends drift toward the shared percept of olfactory white (Weiss et al., 2012). A difference between two mixtures therefore matters more for some odour qualities than for others. $\gamma$ is a direction in Pony's pre-learned embedding: from the ratings it learns along which odour qualities differences between mixtures should be stretched, and along which shrunk.

This distance sees more than the angle. In Figure 3 both pairs of mixtures are 30° apart, so in Step 1 both are 0.52 apart. With the learned length, the pair pointing along $\gamma$ is pushed to 0.92 and the pair pointing away is pulled to 0.29: the same angle now counts for more in some regions of the embedding and for less in others. Expanding the squared distance shows how length and angle combine:

$$d(A,B)^2=\underbrace{\big(g(A)-g(B)\big)^2}_{\text{length difference}}+\underbrace{2\,g(A)\,g(B)\,\big(1-\cos(\hat z(A),\hat z(B))\big)}_{\text{angle, scaled by both lengths}}$$

This is the lever Observation 2 needs. Averaging pulls large mixtures toward the same middle direction, where the angles between them are tiny and Step 1 calls them too alike. If the fit points $\gamma$ toward that region, mixtures there grow long and their small angles turn into larger distances again; whether and how far is decided by the ratings. Only the variation of the length across directions matters: a length equal for every mixture would only rescale $d(A,B)$, which the slope $a$ absorbs. With $\gamma=0$ every length is one and the distance compares directions only, with the learned shares of Objective 1.

2.5 Loss and two-stage training

Pony is trained in two stages, each on the data that can support it. The many single-molecule labels train the network, and the few mixture ratings tune only a small number of parameters on top of it.

Stage 1: train the network on single molecules, then freeze it. The network of Section 2.3, including its atom scorer $u_0$, is trained to predict the 138 odour words of 3,975 labelled molecules, with 1,008 held out. We use open-POM's loss, a class-weighted cross-entropy over the words, and its training recipe: Adam, 62 epochs, batch size 128, learning rate $10^{-3}$ halved every 640 steps, and small L1 and L2 penalties ($10^{-5}$). Ten copies are trained from different random starts. The network is then frozen, so no mixture rating ever changes it. Fine-tuning its 0.117 M parameters on 820 rated pairs would invite the memorization of Section 2.1, and every rated mixture is built from the same 234 molecules.

Stage 2: fit the mixture parameters on DREAM. Each DREAM pair $(A,B)$ comes with a rating $y(A,B)$, the perceived difference between the two mixtures on the 0-to-1 scale of Section 1.1. The predicted rating $\hat y(A,B)$ is a linear function of the distance $d(A,B)$. We fit the model by the mean squared error over the $N$ training pairs (all 820 for the test sets, fewer within a cross-validation fold), with an L2 penalty on the parameters fitted on mixtures:

$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(A,B)}\big(\hat y(A,B)-y(A,B)\big)^2+\lambda\big(\beta^2+\kappa^2+\|\delta\|^2+\|\gamma\|^2\big)$$

$\beta$ and $\delta$ are the scale and the correction of the atom scorer (Section 2.3), $\kappa$ is the weight of the odd-one-out cosine and $\gamma$ the length vector (Section 2.4), and $\lambda=10^{-3}$ is the weight of the penalty. $\delta$ and $\gamma$ have 64 entries each and are fitted on at most 820 rated pairs, so the penalty shrinks them toward their defaults: $\delta=0$ keeps the direction of the single-molecule scorer, with $\beta$ setting its scale (as in L2-SP, Li et al., 2018), and $\gamma=0$ gives every mixture length one. $\beta$ and $\kappa$ get the same small penalty; the slope $a$, intercept $c$ and temperature $\tau$ are not penalized. The network stays frozen. In all, 133 numbers are fitted: $a$, $c$, $\beta$, $\kappa$, $\tau$ and the 64 entries each of $\delta$ and $\gamma$. They are fitted separately for each of the ten copies from Stage 1, and the ten predicted ratings of a pair are averaged.

The loss is minimized with L-BFGS (Liu and Nocedal, 1989) and a strong Wolfe line search, for up to 300 iterations. It starts with $\beta$, $\kappa$, $\delta$ and $\gamma$ at zero and $\tau$ at one, so all molecules weigh the same and every mixture has length one; $a$ and $c$ start from least squares.

Which parts matter most

Reading the two tables together:

  1. The L2 penalty is needed for the fitted parts to generalize. Pony fits 133 numbers on 820 rated pairs, and 128 of them sit in two 64-entry vectors, $\delta$ and $\gamma$. Without a penalty these vectors fit the noise in the ratings: T46 falls from 0.593 to 0.339, V50 from 0.622 to 0.405, and cross-validation collapses from 0.464 to 0.009, because in many folds the length runs away on the training pairs. This is the largest drop in Table 8. The penalty pulls each vector toward the value that switches it off (Krogh and Hertz, 1991); for $\delta$ that value keeps the direction of the single-molecule scorer (L2-SP, Li et al., 2018). A vector then moves only as far as the ratings consistently ask.

  2. Atom attention helps in two roles: pooling inside the network, and strength in the mixture. Inside the network, attention pools the atom vectors into the molecule vector, and in this role it improves every column: T46 from 0.442 to 0.451, V50 from 0.570 to 0.578 and cross-validation from 0.395 to 0.399 (Table 6). Its larger role is in the mixture. In a blend some odorants dominate and others are partly masked (Cain, 1975; Thomas-Danguin et al., 2014), partly because odorants compete at shared receptors (Reddy et al., 2018). Pony's attention was trained on 3,975 single molecules to find the atoms that drive a molecule's odour, and the same atom scores give each molecule its strength. Used as weights with only the slope and intercept fitted, they already lift T46 to 0.467 and V50 to 0.593. Built up, the single scale $\beta$ then adds 0.028 on T46, 0.024 on V50 and 0.015 in cross-validation, more per fitted number than any other part, and with the correction $\delta$, $\tau$ it gives about two-thirds of Pony's gain over Step 1 on T46 and in cross-validation (Table 7). Removing strength altogether ($\beta$, $\delta$ and $\tau$) costs 0.114 on T46 and 0.027 in cross-validation, the largest losses of any part apart from the penalty (Table 8). Attention therefore supplies the strength signal that the mixture readout relies on.

  3. The mixture length helps on every test set. Large blends drift toward a shared percept (Weiss et al., 2012), so where a blend points says something about how distinct it still is. The length $g(A)$ lets $d(A,B)$ use this, stretching distances in some regions of the embedding and shrinking them in others. Adding it raises T46 by 0.042, V50 by 0.023 and cross-validation by 0.019. Because the length is the last part built up, Tables 7 and 8 make the same comparison here.

  4. The anchored correction helps T46 and costs V50. The single-molecule scorer was trained to describe a molecule's odour, not to rank molecules within a blend. The correction $\delta$ lets the mixture ratings tilt it, and $\tau$ sets how far the strongest atom alone decides. Built up, these 65 numbers add 0.029 in cross-validation and 0.068 on T46. Removed, T46 drops by 0.062 and cross-validation by 0.010, but V50 rises from 0.622 to 0.646. The two test sets disagree here, and the V50 cost grows with the mixture length: without $\gamma$ the correction costs V50 0.004 (Table 7), with it 0.024 (Table 8). With 64 free entries tilting the scorer, it is the strength part fitted most directly to the DREAM ratings, and its gain carries over to T46 and to held-out development mixtures but not to V50.

  5. The odd-one-out term and the anchored correction act independently. Both decide which molecule leads a mixture beyond what the frozen single-molecule scorer says, from two different sides. The correction $\delta$ looks at a molecule's own atoms. The odd-one-out term $\kappa$ looks at its company: mixtures of similar odours have been proposed to be more likely perceived as one whole (reviewed by Thomas-Danguin et al., 2014), so a molecule unlike the rest of its blend may count differently; the sign of $\kappa$ is fitted, not assumed. Removed alone, $\kappa$ lowers cross-validation from 0.464 to 0.458 and V50 from 0.622 to 0.618, and leaves T46 level (0.594). Removed together with the correction, the two losses add up: cross-validation falls to 0.449 and T46 to 0.527, while V50 rises to 0.642 as it does when the correction alone is removed. The model without either fits half the numbers (67) and is still above every baseline in Table 4, but it gives up 0.066 on T46 and 0.015 in cross-validation. Fitted on DREAM, these two parts sharpen the shares on the development mixtures and on T46. The one number $\kappa$ costs is repaid on every set but T46, where it is level.

Read together, the two tables agree on the main points. Strength from atom attention and the mixture length improve every column in both. The anchored correction is the largest single step on T46 and in cross-validation but costs V50, so it is the part to watch when more mixture data arrive. The penalty, tested only in Table 8, is necessary.

2.8 Future work

Pony is shaped by how little mixture data exist: 820 rated pairs of 800 mixtures. With more rated mixtures, the network itself could be fine-tuned on mixtures rather than kept frozen, which may improve the results further. More data would also support the parts we left out: self-attention between atoms, for long-range and case-by-case interactions inside a molecule (Section 2.3), and learned interactions between particular molecules, such as one molecule suppressing a specific partner.

Conclusion

Predicting the perceptual similarity of odour mixtures is a low-data problem: a few thousand labelled molecules and 820 rated mixture pairs. In this regime a large pretrained odour model tends to memorize its training molecules, and averaging its predicted word profiles treats all components alike. We showed that structure, rather than scale, improves prediction. Pony is a compact graph neural network, trained on single-molecule odour labels and then frozen. Its mixture readout is additive and follows a principle from olfactory psychophysics: components contribute unequally, and a few dominant ones outweigh the rest. Their weights are read from the network's attention over atoms. Each mixture embedding also carries a learned length, so the distance between two mixtures depends on both the direction and the length of their embeddings. Only a small number of regularized parameters are fitted on mixtures. On the DREAM mixture challenge data, Pony improves on the open-POM baseline by 37% on T46, 13% on V50 and 18% in cross-validation, and it outperforms all prior methods we evaluated. As more rated mixtures become available, we plan to model finer-grained interactions between atoms within a molecule and between molecules within a mixture.

What open-POM can do without

We retrained open-POM's layout with one part changed at a time (Table A3). Its LSTM pooling can be removed: a plain average over atoms keeps accuracy, gap and mixture scores unchanged with 0.88 M fewer parameters. One learned query over atoms, as Pony uses, does slightly better than the LSTM on every mixture set (T46 0.404 → 0.457, V50 0.554 → 0.570, CV 0.397 → 0.404). The number of message-passing rounds hardly matters, and halving or doubling the width changes nothing. Only replacing the deep read-out with a linear layer lowers the memorization gap, from 0.069 to 0.058.
